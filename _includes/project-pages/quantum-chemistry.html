Three hard molecules, and how close we are to solving them.

The Quantum Advantage Tracker lists three molecules where quantum computers are expected to win: nitrogen pulling apart, and two iron–sulfur clusters found in proteins. We ask what the lowest energy is that anyone can actually prove, by any method, on the tracker’s exact problem files.

So far the best answers on all three are classical. We have solved one exactly, lead on another, and trail the best large classical calculation on the third.

01

What “solving” a molecule means

Chemistry is decided by the ground-state energy: the lowest energy the electrons can settle into. Get it right and you can predict how strongly bonds hold and which reactions happen. Each benchmark fixes the problem exactly: a set of orbitals and a file of electron–electron interaction integrals, the Hamiltonian.

Every method here writes down a trial wavefunction and measures its energy. A basic rule of quantum mechanics says that energy can never be below the true answer. So lower is always better, and a lower number from a stored wavefunction is a proof that the true answer is at least that low.

The catch is size. The exact wavefunction is a list of weights, one for every way of placing the electrons in the orbitals. That list grows explosively, which is why quantum computers are proposed and why classical methods compress it instead.

Units. Energies are in hartree (Eh). 1 mEh is a thousandth of a hartree, about 0.63 kcal/mol. Chemists call 1.6 mEh “chemical accuracy”. 1 µEh is a thousandth of a mEh.

2Fe–2S: solved exactly

A two-iron cluster with 30 electrons in 20 orbitals. With the spin fixed there are 240,374,016 configurations, few enough to keep every one. We solved the full problem directly and got −116.60560912 Eh.

That number matches the exact value in the HI-VQE paper and DMRG at bond dimension 8,000, which also confirms we are solving the same Hamiltonian as everyone else. From here on, any method can be graded against the true answer instead of against another approximation.

4Fe–4S: ahead of quantum, behind the biggest classical run

The cubane has 54 electrons in 36 orbitals, far beyond what can be stored exactly. The first lesson was about orbitals: in the tracker’s default orbitals, DMRG stays stuck 0.4–1.0 Eh too high. Placing orbitals on individual iron and sulfur atoms fixed that and gave −327.2395754 Eh at bond dimension 3,000. The second lesson is that what remains is mostly bond dimension.

06

Do the quantum samples help?

The leading quantum-assisted method, sample-based quantum diagonalization (SQD), runs a circuit on a quantum computer, reads out bitstrings, and treats each one as a configuration to keep. A classical computer then solves the problem inside that set. The question is whether the hardware picks better configurations than something cheaper would.

Scrambled bitstrings do as well as the real ones, and choosing configurations from the Hamiltonian alone does 2.5–11× better at the same size. On 4Fe–4S the gain comes from the classical recovery loop, not from the hardware.

Headline SQD numbers are often extrapolated to an infinitely large set. On 2Fe–2S we know the exact answer, so we can test that. The fitted curve lands below the exact energy, which no wavefunction can do. It is also too high at the point where the set already contains every configuration.

Our own emulated runs (Quantinuum Helios emulator, no real hardware) tell the same story: the samples add nothing over a classical selection of equal size. For a quantum result to matter here, it has to beat an equally strong classical workflow of the same size. None has yet.

08

How every number is checked

  • Same problem

    Each Hamiltonian file is pinned by its SHA-256 hash and shown equal to the tracker’s file, up to an orbital rotation, to 10⁻¹² Eh.

  • Stored state

    Every energy belongs to a saved wavefunction, not a number printed mid-run.

  • Fresh replay

    A separate process reloads the state and recomputes the energy from the written integrals. All agree to better than 10⁻¹² Eh.

  • Right spin

    Electron count, spin projection and ⟨S²⟩ are recorded for every state.

01 / The arithmetic

Where Shor spends its gates

Shor finds factors by looking for a repeating pattern in powers taken modulo the RSA number. Computing those powers takes most of the work. In Gidney’s low-qubit circuit, initial lookups and one cleanup stage account for about 85% of the Toffoli gates, which are costly on an error-corrected quantum computer.

With the other settings fixed, sharing across three primes cut the estimated Toffoli count by 28.8%. The circuit also keeps some intermediate values in its workspace so it can clear temporary registers without recalculating those values.

The arithmetic can then carry larger values forward and take the remainder once at the end. The paper proves that, when the registers are wide enough, these changes preserve the reference circuit’s output and relative quantum phases.

03 / What this means for hardware

When do fewer gates save time?

A quantum computer also has to move data, correct errors, and supply resources for gates. Sharing a lookup reduces the gate count but makes its output wider. How much time that saves depends on what the machine can handle efficiently.

Surface code · Faster with more qubits

In our model, the six-prime circuit takes 1.46 days at 1.33 million physical qubits. The reference takes 3.48 days at 980,000 qubits.

Neutral atoms · Faster within the same footprint

In the balanced model, the two-prime circuit takes 22.9 years versus 28.3 years at 13,255 atoms. The modeled runtime is still measured in decades.

What the work adds to Shor

It gives hardware designers a cheaper implementation of Shor’s modular arithmetic and a way to choose how much workspace to trade for fewer gates. The next step is to compile the circuits for a specific architecture and check whether the projected savings hold once data movement and scheduling are included.

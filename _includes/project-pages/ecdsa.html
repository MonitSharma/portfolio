ECDSA.fail scores a single reversible point addition on Bitcoin’s curve. I built a converter that turns that addition into a complete, gate-counted Shor circuit for recovering a private key.

I ran it on my own circuit and thirteen others from the public leaderboard. At the same qubit budget, the converted circuits need 1.55–2.30× fewer Toffolis than the published designs.

01

The challenge and my entries

Each entry is a reversible circuit for one secp256k1 point addition with a classical offset. The organisers simulate it on 9,024 inputs and record peak logical qubits and mean executed Toffoli gates. Fewer of both is better, so the public results form a frontier that moved quickly through October and November.

Hold an intermediate result

My low-gate circuit found thirteen places where an idle wire could keep a Boolean product until it was needed again. One Toffoli computes it, CNOTs copy and reuse it, and a final CNOT returns the wire to zero. That circuit reached 746,089 Toffolis at 1,464 qubits.

Simplify classical controls

A separate 1,234-qubit circuit simplified the classical conditions that decide which gates run, checking each Boolean identity over every input it tracks. It held the best product score on 19–20 October with 1,234 × 761,077. 02

From one addition to a full attack

To find a private key k from a public key Q = k·G, Shor’s algorithm prepares two 256-bit exponent registers and computes a·G + b·Q in superposition. It does this a window of w bits at a time. Each window looks up a precomputed point in a table and adds it to an accumulator, so a run needs 2⌈256/w⌉ − 1 additions.

1 · Lift the offset

A leaderboard circuit adds a fixed classical point. In Shor the point depends on the window, so the converter loads its bits from a table into wires the circuit leaves idle. On my circuit this costs 13 extra Toffolis and no extra qubits.

2 · Avoid the bad cases

Affine addition breaks when the two points are equal or opposite. Each table entry is shifted by a known offset, chosen without using the key, so these cases become rare. The shifts are removed classically at the end.

3 · Count everything

The total adds every lookup, the first window and the final Fourier transform. Runs that fail are thrown away, so the cost is divided by the measured chance that a run is kept.

The full-attack frontier

Each converted circuit and window gives one full attack. The staircase keeps the cheapest attack within each logical-qubit budget. The published designs from Google (Babbush et al., 2026) and Schrottenloher (2026) are assembled with the same formula from their per-addition counts. That formula reproduces their published totals exactly.

What it would mean on hardware

These are projections for machines that do not exist yet. Each model prices Toffolis and lookups from published operation costs and applies the same rules to every circuit, without routing or layout. They are useful for comparing circuits, not for predicting a date.

06

What is new here, and what is not

Mostly the community’s savings

The gap to the published designs comes from the per-addition circuits that ECDSA.fail participants built. The full-attack formula is Schrottenloher’s. What this project adds is the conversion: lifting the offset into a lookup, choosing offsets without the key, counting every gate of the full run, and checking each circuit on fresh inputs and toy curves.

Ideas that did not carry over

The tricks from my RSA work did not help here. Lookups are only about 8% of an addition, so sharing them saves little. Checkpoints found no reusable pairs and lazy reduction saved under 0.1%. A residue-number approach was estimated at 240.5 Toffolis and dropped. P-256 is a model estimate only.

This is a portfolio write-up of unreviewed work. The converter, data and a short note are not yet public.
